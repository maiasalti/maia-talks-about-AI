## NVIDIA's Crash
On January 27th in 2025, NVIDIA lost 589 billion US dollars in market cap in one day, the largest one-day loss for any company in US history, beating its own prior record of 279 billion in September of 2024. The broader tech/AI market lost about 1 trillion USD that day.

## Compute
Initially a company that sold computer chips for game rendering, a process that requires large amounts of mathematical computation, NVIDIA now sells the companies that create the frontier models GPUs that can provide the compute they need to function at the level of capability that they do. Compute has been a scarce asset for many of these competing companies (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta).

## The Scaling Law
The relation with compute and model capability has always been known to be predictable.

<ScalingCurveTable />

If you increase compute, model capability will increase. This is what led to companies focusing on just making the model much bigger: feeding them more data, providing more compute, and adding more parameters.

## The Consequences of Believing The Scaling Law
Based on the assumption that the scaling law was the only truth, the U.S. controlled export of advanced GPUs to deny frontier AI to other countries, like China). The market also adjusted to this truth, which skyrocketed NVIDIA’s valuation, because of how much compute it provided to the US AI companies (GPT-3 trained on NVIDIA V100, Llama 2 on A100, Claude 2 on A100)

## U.S. Export Control
DeepSeek, the Chinese AI company trying to compete with the US frontier models, didn’t have the same access to the GPUs: they were trained on NVIDIA’s H800s, which is a China-specific version of the H100 designed to comply with the U.S. export restrictions, which had reduced interconnect performance (meaning slowed communication between GPUs, which matters when training large models across thousands of GPUs).

## How Scarcity Forced Efficiency

__Mixture of Experts__

A normal model runs every parameter for every token it processes. If the model has 175 billion parameters (GPT-3), it will need to run all 175 billion of those parameters for each word, which of course you can assume to be very computationally expensive.

DeepSeek, constrained by compute, sought to work inside of the box. They focussed on the Mixture-of-Experts model (MoE) which, instead of running all parameters for every token, picks only a few relevant “expert” parameters to activate, and leaves the rest asleep. So DeepSeek-V3 has about 671 billion total parameters in memory but only about 37 billion fire per token (5%). The payoff is that you get the knowledge capacity of a huge model while only paying the compute cost of a smaller one.

The analogical equivalent would be if a patient broke their arm and went to see every specialist in the hospital (GPT-3) vs. only seeing an orthopedist (DeepSeek).

__Multi-head Latent Attention__

When a model creates text, it has to keep running notes of everything it’s already processed and written (also called KV-cache). For long sequences this takes up a lot of memory, which matters a lot when the GPU that you have, which is slower at communicating, needs to move all of this data around.

MLA compresses the running notes so that data transferring becomes much easier and more efficient. This helped DeepSeek with the memory bandwidth problem and was known to have introduced this method.

## DeepSeek Defied

So DeepSeek, although still spent compute on pre-training, spent more compute generating a long internal chain of reasoning, allowing the model to think through complex queries rather than using a “larger pre-training brain,” and they did that through reinforcement learning (RL). It’s the equivalent of making a child memorize a textbook (pre-training-heavy compute) versus giving a child practice quizzes and telling them where they were wrong and right (reinforcement learning for inference).

## Conclusion

So did DeepSeek break the scaling law? Not exactly. They showed that capability could come from variable inference-time compute and not just fixed pre-training compute, following the path o1 opened, but they did it cheaply via RL.

<CapabilityCostChart />

NVIDIA crashed in January of 2025 because the market thought that compute was no longer the moat once DeepSeek was released, and therefore the demand of GPUs decreased. But that was wrong, compute was just distributed differently. If anything, it increases the demand for compute because a pre-training cost is paid once, whereas an inference cost is paid every single time anyone uses the model. So even though they made compute cheaper, it has potential to increase the demand.

So the question that I have to ask is this: (export controls aside) would it be better for NVIDIA to sell chips to China, accepting the risk that abundant compute might let them reach the frontier first, with all the dangers that implies too, or to restrict access and risk one of two things: that scarcity drives China to build its own competitive GPUs, or forces the kind of efficiency innovation that reaches the frontier anyway?
